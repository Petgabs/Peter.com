About me

≡About me

I believe that mathematics is best learned in an environment where students feel safe enough to think clearly and brave enough to make mistakes. That is why I cultivate a classroom built on warmth, approachability, and genuine human connection — because a student who is nervous cannot learn deeply, and a student who feels seen will try boldly. I am not the teacher who stands at the front and lectures from a distance; I am the teacher who kneels beside a desk, listens intently, and asks, "Where are you stuck, and how can we move forward together?" My teaching is rooted in respect — not the kind that is demanded, but the kind that is earned through consistent kindness, patience, and high expectations. I believe that when I show my students genuine care and dignity, they respond with trust and effort. This trust becomes the foundation for everything else: differentiated instruction that honours each learner's unique pace and style, scaffolding that meets them where they are and gently stretches them beyond, and feedback that builds confidence rather than deflates it. Every student arrives with a different story, a different strength, and a different struggle — and my job is to meet each one where they stand. Ultimately, my philosophy is simple: teach the student before you teach the mathematics. When relationships come first, motivation follows. When safety is guaranteed, curiosity flourishes. And when respect is mutual, growth becomes inevitable. This is not just how I teach — it is how I show up, every single day.

⚖Teaching philosophy

My teaching philosophy is rooted in a simple truth: students learn best when they feel safe, seen, and supported. I create classrooms where anxiety fades and curiosity thrives, where instruction is as unique as the individuals receiving it, and where scaffolding turns struggle into steady progress. I invest in relationships because connection fuels motivation. I lead with respect because dignity begets dignity. Every lesson I teach is guided by these beliefs — not as theory, but as practice.

A Journey of Growth: Learning from Australia's Classrooms

Recently, I embarked on a professional pilgrimage that I had long envisioned — a journey to Australia, not as a tourist, but as a learner. This was not merely a trip; it was an intentional investment in my growth as an educator and leader. I had the privilege of immersing myself in two distinguished institutions: PLC Armidale and PLC Sydney, each offering a unique window into exemplary educational practices. My two-week immersion at PLC Armidale was transformative. I observed classrooms across different year levels, witnessing how skilled teachers engage students, scaffold learning, and foster intellectual curiosity. I absorbed the school culture — the unspoken values, the rituals of respect, the seamless integration of care and rigor. I sat in academic leadership meetings, listening to how decisions are made, how data informs practice, and how a shared vision unites an entire school community. Every observation deepened my understanding of what world-class education looks like. From Armidale, I traveled to PLC Sydney, where I continued this rich learning experience. Once again, I observed classes, engaged with teachers, and participated in discussions about curriculum, assessment, and student well-being. I saw different approaches to similar challenges, and I reflected on how these strategies could be adapted to my own context. The school cultures I experienced were distinct yet equally inspiring — both grounded in excellence, both committed to nurturing the whole student. These experiences were more than professional development; they were moments of profound reflection. I saw classrooms where students were not passive recipients but active architects of their learning. I saw teachers who were not just content experts but facilitators of curiosity. I saw leaders who were not just administrators but visionaries who inspired those around them. And I asked myself: How can I bring these lessons home? How can I translate these observations into action? What I treasure most from this journey is not just the strategies I learned, but the perspective I gained. Teaching is not confined by borders; it is a universal calling that adapts to culture, context, and community. Yet, there is always something to learn from how others answer the same questions we face: How do we motivate? How do we differentiate? How do we lead with both heart and high standards? This experience has reaffirmed my commitment to lifelong learning. It has reminded me that great teachers never stop being students. And it has equipped me with fresh ideas, renewed energy, and a deeper appreciation for the craft of teaching. I returned home not just with notes and photos, but with a transformed mindset — ready to apply what I have learned and eager to share it with my colleagues and students.

Self-Discovery: The First Step Toward Growth

One of the most meaningful programs we have established at our school is Student Mentorship — a structured yet personal space where students feel seen, heard, and supported. At the heart of this program lies a simple but powerful activity: self-discovery. The purpose of this session was to guide students inward — to pause, reflect, and honestly assess who they are before they could decide who they want to become. Each student received a thoughtfully designed worksheet with guiding questions that encouraged deep, personal reflection. They explored their core advantages, identified one small area for improvement, articulated their genuine interests, and looked back at their previous semester's goal — asking themselves honestly: Did I achieve it? Why or why not? This was not about judgment. It was about awareness. Students were invited to confront their successes and setbacks with kindness, to recognise patterns in their effort and outcomes, and to understand that growth begins with honesty. The session culminated in two meaningful moments. First, each student wrote a personal goal for the upcoming semester — not a vague wish, but a concrete commitment rooted in their self-discovery. Second, they gathered in groups to share their reflections, creating a culture of trust and mutual encouragement. Listening to one another, they realised they were not alone in their struggles or aspirations. By the end of the session, students walked away with more than a worksheet — they carried a clearer sense of direction, a renewed motivation to grow, and the quiet confidence that comes from truly knowing oneself. This is the heart of mentorship: helping students discover their own light, then guiding them to shine.

Learning Beyond Walls: My Story of Collaborative Growth

I have always believed that mathematics is not a subject to be mastered in isolation — it is a discipline best explored together. That is why collaborative learning sits at the heart of my teaching practice. In my classroom, students are regularly grouped into small teams, each tasked with solving problems assigned to them. This is not just about dividing work; it is about building a community of thinkers who learn from and with one another. What I love most about this strategy is its power to reach every learner — especially the quiet ones. In a traditional lecture setting, shy students often hesitate to raise their hands, fearing judgment or embarrassment. But in small groups, they find the courage to ask questions, share ideas, and seek clarification from their peers. The dynamic shifts. The pressure dissolves. And suddenly, learning becomes a shared journey rather than a solitary struggle. But I did not stop there. Over the years, I noticed something: when students spend long hours inside the four walls of a classroom, their energy wanes, their eyes grow heavy, and their focus falters. So I made a simple but intentional decision — I began taking my students outside. In our school, there is an open space with tables and chairs designed as a study area — a perfect place for a differentiated learning environment. I brought the same set of problems to this different setting, and the transformation was remarkable. The same students who seemed drowsy indoors became alert, engaged, and enthusiastic outdoors. The open space and he change of scenery — it reignited their curiosity and renewed their energy. They tackled problems with a vigour that I rarely saw inside the classroom. This experience confirmed my belief: environment matters. A change in setting is not just a novelty — it is a powerful instructional tool that can awaken a student's desire to learn. This approach, for me, is an extension of differentiated instruction. It is not about lowering standards or changing content — it is about changing the conditions under which students learn best. Some thrive in structured indoor settings; others flourish when given freedom and fresh air. By offering both, I ensure that every student has an opportunity to succeed. Collaborative learning, outdoor lessons, and differentiated environments — these are not just strategies I use. They are reflections of who l am as a teacher: someone who believes that learning should be alive, connected, and responsive to the needs of every learner.

Checkmate: A Story of Passion, Mentorship, and Community

My love for chess began long before I became a teacher. Even in high school, I was drawn to the quiet intensity of the game — the thrill of strategy, the beauty of foresight, and the discipline of thinking several moves ahead. I had the privilege of representing our school in various local and provincial chess tournaments, experiences that sharpened my skills and deepened my appreciation for the game. When I became an educator, I knew I wanted to share this passion with my students. So I created a Chess Club — a space where students could learn, play, and grow together. The response was overwhelming. Every Tuesday afternoon, eager students gathered to explore the game, from learning the basics to refining advanced strategies. The club quickly became a vibrant community of thinkers, problem-solvers, and friends. To celebrate their progress and foster a spirit of friendly competition, we conducted a school-wide chess tournament. It was a moment of pride — watching students apply their skills, demonstrate sportsmanship, and experience the joy of both victory and graceful defeat. As the club grew, I introduced a new layer of leadership: I invited my advanced chess players to mentor the newer members. This peer-teaching approach not only reinforced their own understanding but also cultivated a culture of generosity, patience, and camaraderie. The advanced players became teachers, the beginners became eager learners, and the club became more than a place to play — it became a community built on mutual respect and shared growth. Through the Chess Club, I witnessed something beautiful: students discovering not just the game, but their own capacity for strategic thinking, resilience, and collaboration. Chess taught them that every move matters, that patience pays off, and that learning is a lifelong journey — whether on the board or in life.

A Jǐn Qí – A Parent's Gift of Gratitude and Respect

In Chinese tradition, few gestures carry as much weight as the giving of a Jǐn Qí (锦旗). This ceremonial banner, made of rich maroon fabric with gold lettering, is not a casual gift — it is a formal expression of deep respect and heartfelt gratitude, reserved for those who have made a profound difference in another's life. The symbolism of the Jǐn Qí is found in its very colours. The maroon represents warmth, sincerity, and honour — the deepest red conveying the most genuine thanks. The gold signifies excellence and lasting value, elevating the message to one of prestige and enduring recognition. Together, they create a tribute that is both visual and emotional, meant to be displayed with pride. I was humbled and deeply moved when a parent presented me with a Jǐn Qí inscribed with the words: "Meticulous in teaching, attentive in guiding." This parent, whose child I had the privilege of teaching, chose this traditional and formal gesture to express their gratitude for my dedication, patience, and care. Unlike an official certificate from an institution, this banner came from the heart of a family. It spoke not of grades or curriculum, but of the quiet, lasting impact a teacher can have on a young life — and the trust that families place in us. Today, that Jǐn Qí hangs in my space as a daily reminder that teaching is not just a profession; it is a privilege, a responsibility, and a gift.

Students Are Unpredictable: The Silent Efforts Behind Success

In February 2026, I was invited as the teacher representative of the international division to deliver a speech celebrating a historic achievement in our school's HSC program. Four of my students achieved Band 6, the highest possible rank in the HSC State Exam, and one student, Sherry, attained an ATAR of 99.9% — the highest ATAR in the history of our school's HSC program. Adding to this remarkable milestone, Sherry also achieved 98% in Mathematics Advanced, the highest external exam mark ever obtained in my Mathematics Advanced class. Standing before my fellow colleagues, I shared the journey behind these results — the challenges, the strategies, and the quiet, often invisible efforts that transformed potential into excellence. I opened my speech with a lesson that took me four years to learn: students are unpredictable. In my first year, with few resources, one student achieved a Band 6. Yet, in the following years, despite working harder and creating my own materials, no one reached that top band. This year, however, four students achieved Band 6 and five achieved Band 5 — and some of these high achievers were not the ones I had initially predicted. Their success reminded me that our job is not to predict outcomes, but to create the conditions for potential to bloom. I then shared the five key strategies that formed the backbone of our preparation: Building Mathematical Vocabulary to bridge the language gap; a Differentiated Revision Book that served as a year-long roadmap; a 7-Week External Revision Plan to structure the final stretch; Differentiation for Individuals to meet every learner where they are; and Building Trust and a Safe Environment where students like Sherry — exceptionally independent yet still thriving in an atmosphere of trust and support — felt empowered to take ownership of their learning. Behind this success, I emphasised, are countless silent efforts — late nights marking, weekends designing materials, and the energy spent encouraging discouraged students. Sometimes that effort feels invisible, but this year, seeing four Band 6s, five Band 5s, Sherry's historic 99.9% ATAR, and her 98% in Mathematics Advanced — the highest external exam mark in the history of my class — I felt those efforts were validated. My core realization is this: students are unpredictable, but the teacher's effort must be consistent. We cannot control every result, but we can control our persistence, our care, and our willingness to try new strategies. Our students may not always say thank you or see every sacrifice, but their growth, their confidence, and their results — like Sherry's extraordinary achievements — are the real reflection of our work

Driving Excellence: HSC Results Analysis and Strategic Action Planning

I have always believed that data without action is merely information. That is why, as HSC Coordinator, I have made it a priority to transform our annual HSC results into a catalyst for meaningful improvement. Every year, I convene all subject teachers for an in-depth Results Analysis event. Together, we meticulously examine each subject's performance data, comparing results with the previous year to identify trends, celebrate successes, and pinpoint areas for growth. Through collaborative discussion, we surface key findings — both strengths to amplify and gaps to address — and document these in a formal report with clear, actionable recommendations. But analysis is only the first step. The heart of the event lies in forward planning. Guided by our findings, each teacher develops a 7-Week Intensive External Exam Plan — a structured, targeted revision strategy designed to maximise student readiness in the final stretch. This plan includes focused practice on identified weak areas, timed exam simulations, and strategies to build student confidence and stamina. To close the loop, each teacher presents their analysis and their actionable plan to the group. This presentation fosters accountability, encourages collaborative learning, and ensures that every teacher leaves with both a clear roadmap and the support of their colleagues. Through this systematic, reflective process, we ensure that our HSC results are never just numbers — they are a foundation for growth. We learn, we adapt, and we continuously strive to raise the bar for every student.

From Curiosity to Creation: My AI Learning Journey

It all started with a spark of curiosity. I found myself increasingly drawn to the world of Artificial Intelligence — watching videos, reading articles, and experimenting with tools I had never used before. I wanted to understand not just what AI could do, but how it could help me become a better teacher. My first step was sharing what I learned. I conducted a professional development session for my colleagues, focusing on how AI could boost teaching productivity and how to write effective prompts. I showed them how AI could generate worksheets, design activities, and create meaningful questions — saving time while enhancing quality. But I was not satisfied with just the basics. I wanted to go deeper. I discovered that AI could do more than generate content — it could create interactive tools. Using Arena AI, I designed an HTML-based app that brings the concept of tangent lines to life. With this app, I can drag a tangent line along a curve, change the curve itself, and watch the gradient change in real-time. Suddenly, abstract concepts became visible, tangible, and easy to explain. This experience has transformed my teaching. I can now show students not just what happens, but why it happens — making mathematics more intuitive and engaging. Integrating Language and Content: A CLIL Approach for Bilingual Classrooms

In our ongoing commitment to enhancing bilingual education, I organised a professional development session focused on Content and Language Integrated Learning (CLIL) — a powerful pedagogical approach that combines subject content with language development. To bring this topic to life, l invited two of my esteemed foreign colleagues, Tim and Emilyn, to share their expertise and practical insights. Emilyn opened the session by introducing the core principles of CLIL through the framework of the 4 Cs: Content (the subject matter), Communication (the language needed to access and express that content), Cognition (the thinking skills required for deep understanding), and Culture (the intercultural awareness that enriches learning). Her presentation provided teachers with a clear conceptual foundation, helping them understand how these four elements work together to create meaningful, integrated learning experiences. Building on this foundation, Tim shared a wealth of practical strategies and concrete examples of how teachers can effectively integrate CLIL into their lessons. From scaffolding language for diverse learners to designing tasks that promote both content mastery and linguistic growth, his session offered teachers a toolkit of approaches they could immediately apply in their own classrooms. The session culminated in a collaborative workshop, where teachers were invited to reflect on their own practice and develop a plan for integrating CLIL principles into their upcoming lessons. This hands-on activity encouraged creativity, collaboration, and ownership — ensuring that every participant left not just with new knowledge, but with a clear action plan. By the end of the session, teachers gained both the confidence and the practical tools to make language an integral part of content instruction — not as an add-on, but as a natural and powerful bridge to deeper learning.

Differentiated Instruction: Reaching Every Learner, Every Time

I have always believed that teaching is not about delivering a single lesson to a whole class — it is about reaching every student, wherever they are. This belief inspired me to lead a professional development session on Differentiated Instruction in March 2023. We began by examining the two poles of teaching philosophy: Student-Centered and Teacher-Centered approaches. Teachers reflected on their own practice, recognizing that the most effective classrooms are those that blend structure with flexibility, guidance with autonomy. From there, we explored the foundations of differentiation — Multiple Intelligences and Student Personality. Teachers discovered that students are not empty vessels to be filled, but unique individuals with distinct ways of learning, thinking, and connecting. Understanding this is the first step toward teaching that truly resonates. We then unpacked the four areas where differentiation comes to life: Content, Process, Product, and Learning Environment. Teachers learned that they can differentiate what students learn, how they learn it, how they demonstrate their understanding, and the environment in which learning takes place. To make it real, I shared my own journey with differentiation. I recounted how I have tailored my mathematics instruction to meet diverse needs — from scaffolding for struggling learners to challenging advanced students with enrichment tasks. I also shared a revision book I created, filled with differentiated questions that cater to various learning levels and styles. This resource, I explained, has not only helped my students build confidence but has also made my teaching more intentional and responsive. The session ended with a vibrant discussion, teachers sharing their own ideas and challenges. By the end, participants left with a clear understanding that differentiation is not about creating thirty different lessons — it is about creating a mindset of flexibility, empathy, and high expectations for every learner
